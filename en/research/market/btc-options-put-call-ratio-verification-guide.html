BTC Options Put/Call Ratio Verification: Is the "Bearish Sentiment" Number Real

"Put/Call ratio breaks 0.7, fear rises" — headlines like this are common in crypto media, reducing a single ratio number directly to an overall bullish-or-bearish market verdict. But how a Put/Call ratio is actually calculated is far more nuanced than that conclusion suggests: it can be computed by open interest or by volume, reflecting entirely different time dimensions; more importantly, holding a put option doesn't necessarily mean betting on a price decline — a large share of put positions come from spot holders buying protective puts to hedge downside risk, or from market makers' paired positions under covered-call strategies, neither of which constitutes a directional "bearish" call. Using real BTC options data pulled from Deribit's public API, this guide breaks down how to verify a Put/Call ratio, the common misreading traps, and how to combine it with other metrics for a more accurate sentiment picture. This article discusses verification methodology only and is not investment advice.

  • ✓Cross-verify against expiry distribution and strike concentration rather than drawing a conclusion from one isolated ratio number

1. The Common Playbook Behind Put/Call Ratio Clickbait

The Put/Call ratio is one of the options market's most easily oversimplified metrics for media distribution: headlines like "ratio breaks above X, fear is rising" or "ratio hits a new low, market extremely bullish" essentially compress a statistic loaded with multiple layers of information into a binary sentiment verdict. This kind of simplification spreads well precisely because a single number is far easier for a non-specialist reader to understand and share than the actual structure of options positioning — but the cost is losing a huge amount of critical context: is this ratio an instantaneous snapshot or an average over some window, does the sample cover the whole market or a single exchange, and what is this ratio's normal historical range (an absolute number detached from a historical baseline is meaningless on its own)?

When a researcher encounters this kind of headline, the first instinct shouldn't be to accept or reject the sentiment conclusion, but to first verify how the ratio number itself was calculated, where the data came from, and where this value sits relative to its own historical range. Without that context, a standalone number like "0.7" or "1.2" tells you neither whether the market is bullish or bearish, nor whether it's an unusual reading at all.

  • Media coverage often compresses complex options positioning data into a single ratio, then further compresses that into a binary sentiment verdict, losing critical context.
  • Verification should start by checking the ratio's calculation basis, data source, and sample scope, rather than directly accepting or rejecting the sentiment conclusion.
  • An absolute number detached from its historical normal range is meaningless on its own and can't determine whether the market is in an unusual state.

Real Data

At the same point in time, 24-hour volume showed approximately 33,088.2 BTC in call volume and 15,499.8 BTC in put volume — a Put/Call ratio by volume of approximately 0.468. These figures can be re-verified in real time via Deribit's public API, and will continue to change with market conditions.

This dataset reveals two points worth digging into. First, at the same point in time and for the same underlying asset, the Put/Call ratio calculated by open interest (0.557) and by volume (0.468) come out different — open interest reflects the accumulated state of all currently open contracts built up over some period, while volume reflects the most recent 24 hours of incremental trading activity, closer to a "right now" sentiment reading. If a report doesn't specify which basis it's using, the specific number being cited is already ambiguous before any further sentiment interpretation even begins. Second, both ratios sit below 1, meaning that under either basis, call open interest or volume exceeds put — applying the simplified rule "ratio below 1 means bullish sentiment" without further thought would suggest "the market is broadly optimistic." But that's still only the first step of verification; Sections 3 and 4 explain why you can't stop there.

3. Verification Method One: Nail Down the Calculation Basis — Open Interest vs. Volume, Contract Count vs. Notional Value

Before verifying a Put/Call ratio, the first step is confirming its calculation basis. Open interest reflects the current stock of all still-open contracts, revealing the positioning structure market participants have already built — but that structure could include positions opened weeks or even months ago that remain open, and doesn't necessarily represent the latest shift in market sentiment. Volume reflects new trading activity within a given window (typically 24 hours), better capturing "what just happened," but it's more easily distorted by a single large trade or a batch of expiry-driven closes/opens, making it far more volatile than the open-interest basis. Researchers should check both numbers (as in Section 2's example) rather than citing only one — and when the two diverge noticeably (e.g., the open-interest basis reads bullish while the volume basis reads bearish), that divergence itself is a signal worth investigating further, potentially indicating a shift in market sentiment underway.

Another easily overlooked distinction is whether contracts are summed by count or by notional value. Options contracts typically represent a fixed quantity of the underlying asset per contract (e.g., one BTC option representing 1 BTC); if contracts across different strikes and expiries are simply added by count, that implicitly assumes every contract carries equal "importance" — but deep out-of-the-money and deep in-the-money options don't carry equal weight in terms of actual market impact. A more rigorous verification approach is to calculate the ratio weighted by notional value (contract count × current underlying price) or by delta exposure, which better reflects the real distribution of directional risk exposure rather than a simple stack of contract counts.

  • Open interest reflects stock positioning (possibly old positions); volume reflects the latest incremental activity — both should be checked rather than citing only one.
  • A noticeable divergence between the two bases is itself a signal worth investigating, possibly indicating a sentiment shift underway.
  • Simply summing by contract count implicitly assumes equal weight per contract; weighting by notional value or delta exposure better reflects real directional risk distribution.

4. Verification Method Two: Distinguish Directional Bets From Hedging/Covered Positions

The most easily overlooked — and most critical — layer in interpreting a Put/Call ratio is the actual purpose behind a put position. Holding a put option is intuitively read as "betting on a price decline," but at least two common scenarios have nothing to do with directional bearishness. First, protective puts: spot or futures long holders buy puts to hedge downside risk, while their overall position remains bullish or neutral on the underlying — the put purchase is simply insurance on an existing bullish position, not a bearish stance. Second, under covered-call strategies, market makers or institutions hedging the exposure from calls they've sold will build corresponding hedge positions in the underlying asset or other options — these positions, arising from market-maker risk management, likewise represent no directional market view at all. If an increase in the Put/Call ratio is mostly driven by these two non-directional position types, reading it simply as "bearish sentiment rising" would produce a conclusion opposite to reality.

A further verification step researchers can try is observing whether the ratio's movement is synchronized with changes in spot/futures open interest — if an increase in put positions coincides closely with an increase in spot long positioning, it's more likely protective hedging rather than an independent bearish bet. It's also worth checking the concentration of strike prices: if newly added put positions are heavily concentrated at strikes far below the current price (a typical characteristic of tail-risk hedging), that looks more like "black swan insurance" than a directional call on a near-term decline — a very different market implication from put positions concentrated near the money, which are more likely aimed at capturing short-term directional gains.

  • Protective puts (spot longs hedging with puts) and market-maker hedge positions under covered-call strategies don't constitute directional bearish bets.
  • If a rise in the Put/Call ratio is mostly driven by non-directional hedging positions, reading it simply as "rising bearish sentiment" leads to an incorrect conclusion.
  • Cross-check whether ratio movements sync with spot/futures open interest changes, and whether new put positions concentrate at deep out-of-the-money strikes (a tail-risk hedging signature).

5. Verification Method Three: Cross-Reference Against Expiry Distribution and Historical Baselines

Even after the calculation basis and position nature have both been verified, an isolated, current Put/Call ratio number is still missing an important dimension — where that number sits relative to its own normal historical range. Researchers should try to obtain the ratio's time series over some past period (say, the last 3 or 6 months), calculate its historical mean and standard deviation, and place the current reading within that range — only then can you judge whether the current reading is genuinely a historically rare extreme, or still within normal fluctuation. Judging in isolation whether "0.557 is high or low," detached from a historical baseline, is meaningless.

Additionally, the distribution of options expiries is an important verification dimension: ratio changes in near-dated contracts (expiring within a week, say) more often reflect short-term, event-driven hedging or speculative demand (an upcoming macro data release, regulatory news), while changes in longer-dated contracts (three months or more out) better reflect the market's view on medium-to-long-term trends. If a report cites a ratio calculated across "all expiries combined" without separating near-month from far-month contracts, it obscures the difference between short-term noise and medium-to-long-term trend. Researchers should, where possible, bucket the ratio by expiry and interpret it separately, rather than looking only at a single combined total.

  • Obtain the ratio's historical time series, calculate its historical mean and standard deviation, and judge whether the current reading is genuinely a historical extreme.
  • Near-dated contract ratio changes more often reflect short-term, event-driven demand; far-dated contracts better reflect medium-to-long-term trend views.
  • Bucket the ratio by expiry and interpret separately, to avoid a combined total obscuring the difference between short-term noise and longer-term trend.

6. Verification Checklist and Conclusion

Consolidating the preceding sections into a reusable checklist: First, have you confirmed whether a cited Put/Call ratio is calculated by open interest or by volume, and checked both? Second, have you verified whether the calculation sums contracts by simple count or weights by notional value or delta exposure? Third, have you recognized that a ratio's movement may be mostly driven by protective hedging or covered-call positions rather than pure directional bets? Fourth, have you placed the current reading within a historical time-series range for comparison, and examined it bucketed by expiry, rather than looking only at one isolated combined number? Only after verifying these four points can you form a relatively objective judgment of the real sentiment signal an options market is sending, rather than being swayed by a headline like "ratio breaks above X."
