Cross-Exchange Arbitrage Verification: How Much Slippage Eats Into the Quoted Spread

Cross-exchange price-spread arbitrage is one of the most widely circulated strategy narratives in crypto trading: observe a price spread for the same asset between two exchanges, buy on the cheaper one, sell on the more expensive one, and theoretically lock in a risk-free profit. This narrative gets simplified in much beginner content into a simple arithmetic problem — spread percentage minus trading fees equals profit. But this simplification assumes something that almost never holds in reality: that the quoted price you observe is the price you'll actually execute at. Between the moment you spot a spread opportunity and the moment both legs of the trade — the buy and the sell — actually complete on their respective exchanges, a series of factors can make the final net profit significantly lower than the theoretical spread: slippage from insufficient order book depth, price movement during the execution time gap between the two legs, and if rebalancing positions requires moving assets across exchanges, the on-chain or internal transfer delay and fees involved. This piece breaks down the complete execution chain of cross-exchange arbitrage from spotting a spread to final settlement, where the gap between quoted profit and actual net profit specifically comes from, several hidden costs beginners most commonly overlook, and a practical framework for verifying whether an arbitrage strategy is genuinely viable. Verification Checklist

  • ✓Check whether rebalancing positions requires transferring assets across exchanges, and if so, verify the specific transfer time and fee amount
  • ✓Backtest the strategy's net profit under real execution delay using actual historical tick-by-tick trade data — not quote snapshots — rather than the theoretical spread
  • ✓Confirm each exchange's maker/taker fee rate, and whether a volume-based fee discount is actually applicable to your trading volume

1. Quoted Profit vs. Net Profit: Why "Spread Minus Fees" Is a Dangerous Simplification

The starting point for understanding cross-exchange arbitrage's real profitability is recognizing a key distinction: the quote you see on a trading interface only represents how much quantity is available to trade at the current best bid/ask, not that your entire intended trade size can execute at that price. If the size you plan to trade exceeds the quantity resting at the best price level, your order will continue consuming progressively worse-priced levels further down the book, causing your actual average execution price to diverge from the quote you initially saw — this is slippage. The "spread minus fees" simplification completely ignores slippage, essentially assuming infinite order book depth — an assumption that's roughly fine on liquid mainstream pairs but can badly fail on thinner secondary pairs or non-top-tier exchanges, where slippage can erode far more profit than trading fees themselves. A verifier should strictly separate "quoted spread" from "net profit" — the quoted spread is only a preliminary signal for whether an opportunity deserves further verification, not a conclusion ready to act on.

  • The quote on a trading interface only represents quantity available at the best price level; size beyond that executes at progressively worse prices, producing slippage.
  • The "spread minus fees" simplification ignores slippage entirely, effectively assuming infinite order book depth — an assumption that easily fails on thin pairs.
  • The quoted spread should be treated as a preliminary signal for further verification, not a net-profit conclusion ready to act on.

2. Verification Method One: Calculate Real Order Book Depth at the Target Trade Size

Before placing an order, a verifier should directly examine the full order book depth data for the target pair on both exchanges, rather than relying only on the best bid/ask — calculate how many price levels a trade of the planned size would need to consume to fully fill, and use that to estimate the percentage deviation of the actual average execution price from the best quote — this deviation is the slippage cost. A key detail: this calculation needs to be done separately for both legs of the trade (the buy leg and the sell leg), since order book depth between two exchanges is often asymmetric — one side having ample liquidity while the other is thin is a common scenario. A verifier should also recognize that order book depth fluctuates significantly over time, so a slippage estimate calculated at one moment can't be directly applied to future execution windows — for a strategy intended to run repeatedly over the long term, a mechanism for continuously monitoring order book depth changes should be built, rather than relying on a one-time static calculation.

  • Calculate how many order book levels a trade of the planned size would actually need to consume, and estimate the slippage deviation of the real execution price from the best quote accordingly.
  • Order book depth between two exchanges is often asymmetric — the buy leg and sell leg need separate calculations rather than assuming both sides have equal depth.
  • Order book depth fluctuates significantly over time; a strategy run repeatedly long-term needs continuous monitoring rather than a one-time static estimate.

3. Verification Method Two: The Time Cost and Fees of Cross-Exchange Asset Transfers

If an arbitrage strategy requires repeatedly moving assets between exchanges to rebalance positions (e.g., continuously accumulating a long-side asset on one exchange, needing periodic transfers to another exchange to replenish short-side inventory), a verifier must factor this transfer time and fee cost into the complete net profit calculation. On-chain transfers have network confirmation times that vary enormously across different blockchain networks — from seconds to tens of minutes — and some exchanges add their own internal risk-review delay on top; transfer fees may look small per transaction, but accumulate linearly with trade frequency in a high-frequency strategy, potentially becoming a non-trivial fixed cost over time. A verifier should check the historical average transfer time and fee amount for the specific asset between the two exchanges, and factor both costs into the per-trade net profit model based on the strategy's expected execution frequency, rather than treating "asset transfer" as a negligible background operation.

  • The time and fees for cross-exchange asset transfers to rebalance positions must be factored into the complete net profit model, not treated as a negligible background operation.
  • Confirmation times vary enormously across blockchain networks, and some exchanges add their own internal risk-review delay on top — both are real execution costs.
  • Transfer fees accumulate linearly with trade frequency in high-frequency strategies, potentially becoming a non-trivial fixed cost over time.

4. Hidden Cost Checklist: Fee-Discount Thresholds and Price Drift During Execution Delay

A verifier should also check two commonly underestimated hidden costs. First, fee-discount thresholds: most exchanges use tiered maker/taker fee structures where higher trading volume gets lower rates, and many "professional arbitrageur" strategy analyses default to using the lowest fee tier — but if a verifier's own trading volume hasn't reached that tier's threshold, the actual applicable fee rate could be significantly higher, directly eating into the theoretical profit margin. Second, price drift during the time gap between the two order executions: cross-exchange arbitrage requires placing orders on two exchanges separately, and even if both orders are submitted nearly simultaneously, there's still a delay from submission to final execution confirmation ranging from milliseconds to seconds — during this delay window, the underlying asset's price may have already moved, especially during sharp, news-driven volatility, and this price drift can completely wipe out the original spread or even turn it into a loss. A verifier should backtest strategy performance under real execution delay using actual historical tick-by-tick trade data — not quote snapshots — rather than calculating based on an idealized "simultaneous execution" assumption.

  • Fee rates are typically tiered — if a verifier's trading volume hasn't reached the lowest tier's threshold, actual costs could be significantly higher than theoretical estimates.
  • The execution delay between order submission and fill confirmation lets the underlying price drift during that window, potentially wiping out the original spread or turning it into a loss.
  • Backtest strategy performance under real execution delay using actual historical tick-by-tick trade data, rather than an idealized "simultaneous execution" assumption.

5. Cross-Strategy Comparison Framework: Depth Sufficiency, Capital Efficiency, and Execution Stability

When evaluating multiple candidate arbitrage opportunities, a verifier can compare across these dimensions. First, depth sufficiency: is the slippage cost at the target trade size on both sides' order books within an acceptable range, and does depth stay relatively stable over time. Second, capital efficiency: does the strategy require frequent cross-exchange asset transfers, and what proportion of theoretical profit do the transfer time cost and fees consume. Third, execution stability: does this spread opportunity appear frequently and persist long enough to support scaled, repeated execution, or is it merely an occasional, brief window. Fourth, real historical backtest results: how large is the gap between the real net profit margin backtested from tick-by-tick trade data and the theoretical quoted spread — this gap itself is a direct indicator of how much a strategy is "theoretically appealing but practically hollow." Combining these four dimensions produces a well-grounded judgment of an arbitrage opportunity's real viability, rather than concluding from a single percentage number on a quote page.

  • Depth sufficiency, capital efficiency, execution stability, and real historical backtest results are the four key comparison dimensions.
  • The gap between theoretical quoted spread and real backtested net profit margin is a direct indicator of how "theoretically appealing but practically hollow" a strategy is.
  • Combining all four dimensions produces a well-grounded judgment of an arbitrage opportunity's real viability, rather than concluding from a quoted percentage alone.

6. Verification Checklist and Conclusion

Distilling the sections above into a reusable checklist: first, has the real slippage cost at the target trade size on both order books been calculated? Second, has it been checked whether cross-exchange asset transfers are needed, and the specific transfer time and fee amount verified? Third, has the strategy's net profit under real execution delay been backtested using actual historical tick-by-tick trade data? Fourth, has the actual applicable fee tier for one's own trading volume been confirmed, rather than defaulting to the best-case rate? Fifth, has the frequency and duration of this spread opportunity been assessed for whether it supports scaled, repeated execution? Working through these five questions gives a well-grounded judgment of an arbitrage strategy's real profitability, rather than treating an isolated spread percentage on a quote page as if it were realized profit.
