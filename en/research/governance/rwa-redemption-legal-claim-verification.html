After You Burn the Token, What Legal Claim Is Left?

"RWA Tokenization: The On-Chain Token Is Just a Pointer to an Off-Chain Asset" covered the trust structure of issuance and custody: the token is a pointer to an off-chain asset, and the value anchor sits in off-chain law and custody arrangements. This piece goes one step further, into a moment that's often skipped over — the instant a holder actually initiates redemption, burns the token, and demands the underlying asset or its cash value. What do you actually hold at that moment: a direct property right in the underlying asset, an ordinary unsecured claim against the issuer, a contractual claim against a limited-recourse SPV, or just an administrative "we'll process your request" promise with no hard backing behind it? This piece works through five questions: how to find the real redemption mechanism in offering documents rather than marketing pages; how to check whether "bankruptcy remoteness" claims are real; the gap between T+0 promises and money-market-fund-style multi-day settlement reality; what happens to your claim if the issuer becomes insolvent mid-redemption; and which red flags in a project's documentation suggest the redemption right is weaker than advertised. For methodology and learning purposes only — not a judgment on any real protocol, and not investment advice.

  • ✓Confirm which legal position the holder actually occupies at the moment of redemption — direct property right, ordinary unsecured creditor, or limited-recourse contractual claimant against a single SPV — since the three carry entirely different risk levels
  • ✓Verify whether "bankruptcy remoteness" is real: whether the underlying assets have actually been transferred and registered to an independent SPV, whether the SPV's governance documents restrict outside borrowing and guarantees, and whether an independent trustee mechanism and legal opinion back the structure
  • ✓Locate the offering document's specific settlement-timeline commitment and check whether a clause allows delay or batched processing once redemption volume exceeds a threshold, rather than taking T+0 marketing claims at face value

1. Where the redemption clause actually lives: the gap between the marketing page and the offering documents

Most RWA project websites summarize redemption in a single reassuring line — "redeem anytime, fast settlement" — but that sentence isn't a legal document and doesn't itself constitute an enforceable promise. The clauses that actually govern redemption typically sit buried in private placement memoranda, trust or SPV formation documents, and token holder agreements that aren't proactively surfaced on the marketing site, and usually have to be requested directly or located in regulatory filings. Verification means going straight to the "Redemption" or "Repurchase" section of those documents and checking, line by line, how a redemption request is submitted, who holds discretion to approve or reject it, the actual settlement window, and whether redemption can be suspended or delayed — not stopping at the website's one-line summary. If a project only mentions redemption on its marketing page and can't produce a corresponding clause in an offering document, that gap is itself a signal worth taking seriously.

2. What you actually are at the moment of redemption

Burning a token to redeem it puts a holder into one of three legal positions, each carrying a very different risk profile. First, a direct property right in the underlying asset — redemption is exercising an existing entitlement, and the issuer has no real discretion to refuse it. Second, an ordinary unsecured creditor claim against the issuer — the redemption request enters the issuer's liability stack, and if the issuer becomes insolvent, the holder stands in line with other creditors for pro-rata recovery. Third, a contractual claim with recourse limited to a specific SPV — the holder can only assert rights against that SPV's designated assets, with recourse explicitly capped there and unable to reach the issuer's or its affiliates' other assets. These three positions correspond to entirely different contract language, and the first step of verification is finding the clause that states, explicitly, which of the three applies — not assuming the token itself equals a property right.

3. Is "bankruptcy remoteness" real, or just paper?

Issuers routinely use the phrase "bankruptcy remote" to assure holders they won't be dragged down by the issuer's own operating risk, but whether that's actually true depends on several concrete structural features, not a verbal claim. Points worth checking: whether the underlying assets have actually been transferred and registered into an independent SPV, rather than still sitting on the issuer's or an affiliate's own balance sheet; whether the SPV's governing documents restrict it from taking on external debt or guaranteeing third-party obligations, so it can't be pulled into unrelated liabilities; whether there's an independent director or trustee mechanism preventing the issuer from unilaterally disposing of SPV assets; and whether a legal opinion explicitly addresses whether the structure would actually hold up in the issuer's bankruptcy, rather than just describing design intent. If these elements are missing or the documents are vague about them, "bankruptcy remote" is likely marketing language that may not survive real stress.

4. T+0 promises versus money-market-fund-style multi-day settlement reality

Many RWA projects advertise "same-day" or "T+0" redemption, but that description usually applies to ordinary, small, dispersed redemption activity — it doesn't mean the structure itself has genuine instant-settlement capacity. A closer real-world analogy is a money market fund: everyday redemptions clear quickly because the issuer maintains a cash buffer, while the underlying assets — short-term bonds or private credit — often take days or longer to actually liquidate. Once redemption volume exceeds what the cash buffer can cover, actual settlement slides from the advertised T+0 toward the T+2, T+5, or longer windows specified in the offering documents, which are the real settlement-timeline terms, not the marketing site. Verification means locating the document's actual commitment on settlement timing and confirming whether there's a clause allowing delay or batching once redemption volume crosses a threshold — that clause, not the T+0 headline, is what determines real redemption speed.

5. If the issuer goes insolvent mid-redemption, what happens to your claim

One of the most overlooked scenarios is a redemption request that's already been submitted but not yet settled when the issuer suddenly becomes insolvent or enters a bankruptcy process. What happens next depends entirely on the legal position discussed in Section 2: a holder with a direct property right in a genuinely bankruptcy-remote structure should, in principle, see the pending request unaffected and can still assert a claim on the corresponding asset; an ordinary unsecured creditor sees the unsettled redemption amount converted into a general claim in the bankruptcy estate, queued alongside other creditors, with actual recovery potentially far below face value; a holder of a limited-recourse SPV claim depends on whether that SPV itself remains independently solvent — if it does, the claim is largely unaffected, but if the SPV is also caught up in the crisis, the same risk applies. Verifying this scenario means finding the specific clause addressing how submitted-but-unsettled redemption requests are treated if the issuer becomes insolvent.

6. Red flags in the documentation that suggest the redemption right is weaker than advertised

When reading through the documents clause by clause, several patterns of language are worth flagging: redemption language that reserves near-total discretion to the issuer — "at the issuer's sole discretion" or "as the issuer deems appropriate" — instead of stating objective, verifiable approval criteria; settlement timing described only as "as soon as practicable" with no stated number of days or cap; suspension triggers described vaguely, such as "in the event of unusual market conditions," without concrete, verifiable thresholds; bankruptcy-remoteness language that appears only in marketing materials but has no corresponding, binding clause in the formal legal documents; and language that lets the issuer extend the gap between request and settlement indefinitely without any stated cap. None of these alone necessarily indicates bad faith, but the more of them that appear together, the closer the redemption right sits to an administrative promise rather than an enforceable legal entitlement.

7. Summary and a verification checklist

  • Find the redemption clause in the actual offering documents — the private placement memorandum, SPV formation documents, holder agreement — not a one-line marketing-page summary.
  • At the moment of redemption, your legal position is one of three: direct property right, ordinary unsecured creditor, or limited-recourse SPV claimant — each with a different risk profile.
  • "Bankruptcy remote" should be verified against actual asset transfer/registration, SPV borrowing restrictions, independent trustee mechanisms, and a supporting legal opinion.
  • T+0 claims typically apply only to ordinary small redemptions — real settlement timing and delay triggers live in the offering documents, not the marketing site.
  • If the issuer goes insolvent mid-redemption, what happens to an unsettled request depends on your legal position and whether bankruptcy remoteness is genuinely effective.
  • Watch for unlimited issuer discretion, vague settlement timing, unquantified suspension triggers, and bankruptcy-remoteness language that appears only in marketing materials.

Frequently asked: does the word "redeemable" on a website mean the holder has a legally protected redemption right? Not necessarily — it depends on whether the underlying clause is an enforceable entitlement or an administrative promise the issuer retains discretion over. Does a "bankruptcy remote" label mean the structure is risk-free? No — verify that assets were actually transferred and registered, and that the SPV is restricted from taking on outside debt; paper remoteness and effective remoteness are not the same thing. Can a T+0 claim be relied on for real redemption speed? No — rely on the settlement-timeline clause and delay triggers stated in the offering documents instead.
